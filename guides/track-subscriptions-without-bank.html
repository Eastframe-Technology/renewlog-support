How to track subscriptions without connecting your bank

You need a list, about 20 minutes and your last 12 months of statements. Nothing has to be linked to anything. The trade-off is that you do the finding, and you keep the list up to date.

Why people skip the bank link

Apps that detect subscriptions automatically usually connect to your bank through a data provider. That can work well. It also means another company can see your transactions, and you need to trust it, and keep trusting it, with that access.

Some people are fine with that. Others would rather not, or their bank is not supported, or they pay with several cards. For them a list kept by hand does the job.

What you give up

Be honest with yourself about this. A hand-kept list only knows what you tell it. It will not spot a new charge. It will not notice a price rise. If you never look at your statement, a charge can stay hidden. A short check every few months covers most of that risk, and the steps below include one.

Step 1: find everything

Look in these places, in this order. Each one catches things the others miss.

  1. Card and bank statements, 12 months back. One month is not enough, because yearly renewals only show once. Skim for the same merchant repeating, and for small amounts you do not recognise.
  2. Google Play and the App Store. Each keeps its own list. Here is where to find it.
  3. Your email. Search for "receipt", "renewal", "your subscription", "free trial" and "invoice".
  4. Your phone bill. Some services can be billed through your carrier.
  5. Payment apps. PayPal and similar services keep their own list of automatic payments.
  6. Other people in your household. Shared plans are often paid from one person's card and forgotten by everyone else.

Step 2: write down the same six things

  • Name of the service
  • Price, as charged, with tax
  • How often: weekly, monthly, quarterly or yearly
  • Next charge date
  • Which card or account pays it
  • How to cancel: a link, or "Google Play"

The last two save you the most time later. When a card expires you know what breaks, and when you decide to cancel you do not have to hunt for the page.

Step 3: add it up

Mixed billing periods are hard to total in your head. Turn each one into a monthly figure and add them. The subscription cost calculator does that in your browser, with nothing uploaded. Watch the weekly plans in particular: they look small and cost the most over a year.

Step 4: get warned before each charge

A list you only open when you remember is not much use. You need a nudge a few days ahead, while you can still act. There are three ways to do that:

  • A spreadsheet plus calendar events. Free and under your control. You have to create the events and move them yourself.
  • Calendar events alone. A repeating event per subscription. It works, but it gives you no total.
  • A tracker app that does not ask for your bank. RenewLog is ours. You type the entries once, it rolls the dates forward and notifies you one, three or seven days before each charge. The free version covers 5 subscriptions, and a single purchase removes the limit.

Step 5: check it every few months

Put a repeating reminder on your calendar. Then open your latest statement next to your list and look for three things: a charge that is not on the list, a price that has changed, and something you have stopped using. Ten minutes is usually enough. That check is what replaces the automatic detection you gave up.
